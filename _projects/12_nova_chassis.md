**[NOVA Electric Racing](https://novaracing.nl/)** is a student team from TU Delft and Inholland that designs and builds electric racing motorcycles to push what sustainable two-wheeled racing can do. I joined as **Lead Chassis Engineer**, leading a team of six with responsibility for the frame that everything else hangs off — the part that has to survive a rider, a heavy battery, and racing loads without adding weight it does not need.

Leading the chassis meant owning the loop from analysis to a physical frame. I ran **FEM analysis in Ansys across 20+ components**, working through the load cases the frame had to survive and iterating geometry where the stresses said it was too weak or needlessly heavy. The reward was concrete: the finished chassis was validated to carry well over the bike's own working load in test, and the prototype went on to a successful ride-along test — the moment where a frame stops being a simulation and becomes something a person actually trusts their weight to. The bike is being built up gradually, and this was the round where we put the first engine and the new chassis design through their first real test as a system.

One part I enjoyed most was the **bodywork**. Instead of the carbon fibre that race teams reach for by reflex, I developed the outer shell and seat in **flax-fibre composite** — a natural fibre. The honest reason was twofold: a sponsor supplied the flax, and we wanted to do something different, to try a material a bit out of the box rather than defaulting to carbon like everyone else. It is a real trade, not a free win: flax does not match carbon's stiffness-to-weight, so choosing it meant designing around a more modest material rather than papering over it. I fabricated the moulds myself using design-for-manufacturing techniques, building the negative moulds the parts are laid up in.

Two things from NOVA have stayed with me. The first is that taking the lead was not actually the hard part — I liked being the point man; the hard part was carrying it on top of a full course load. What saved me was learning to delegate properly, handing work to teammates according to their individual strengths rather than trying to hold all of it myself. The second is that leading six people on a shared deadline is as much about keeping the interfaces between everyone's parts honest as it is about the engineering itself.
