**[NOVA Electric Racing](https://novaracing.nl/)** is a student team from TU Delft and Inholland that designs and builds electric racing motorcycles to push what sustainable two-wheeled racing can do. I joined as **Lead Chassis Engineer**, leading a team of six with responsibility for the frame that everything else hangs off — the part that has to survive a rider, a heavy battery, and racing loads without adding weight it does not need.

Leading the chassis meant owning the loop from analysis to a physical frame. I ran **FEM analysis in Ansys across 20+ components**, working through the load cases the frame had to survive and iterating geometry where the stresses said it was too weak or needlessly heavy. The reward was concrete: the finished chassis was validated to carry well over the bike's own working load in test, and the prototype went on to a successful ride-along test — the moment where a frame stops being a simulation and becomes something a person actually trusts their weight to.

The decision I am proudest of was the **bodywork**. Instead of the carbon fibre that race teams reach for by reflex, I developed the outer shell and seat in **flax-fibre composite** — a natural fibre — to keep faith with the team's sustainability mission. That is a real trade, not a free win: flax does not match carbon's stiffness-to-weight, so choosing it meant designing around a more modest material rather than papering over it. I fabricated the moulds myself using design-for-manufacturing techniques, building the negative natural-fibre moulds the parts are laid up in.

Two things from NOVA have stayed with me. The first is that leading six people on a shared deadline is as much about keeping the interfaces between everyone's parts honest as it is about the engineering. The second is that a sustainability goal is only real when you let it constrain a material choice you would otherwise have made differently — and then make the design work anyway.
